# East Coast Foam homeowner education source library

Status: working source library / roadmap input  
Audience: East Coast Foam / Lowcountry Digital Works website team  
Purpose: maintain a sourced, homeowner-friendly education backlog without turning competitor marketing claims into ECF facts.

This document is **not** public website copy. It is the research/source layer used to decide what may safely become public content.

## Core editorial rule

A symptom is not a diagnosis.

Conditions such as high humidity, visible mold, musty odor, hot/cold rooms, condensation, or high energy bills can be caused by multiple building-envelope, HVAC, drainage, roofing, plumbing, duct, or insulation problems.

Public ECF content should say:

> "This is a reason to inspect the building envelope and moisture path."

It should **not** say:

> "This proves you need spray foam."

Likewise, visible problems around an existing spray-foam installation can justify professional inspection without automatically proving the foam itself caused the problem.

# 1. Homeowner signs that the building envelope may need attention

## Comfort / energy symptoms

Useful homeowner-facing signs:

- rooms that stay much hotter or colder than the rest of the house;
- large temperature differences between floors;
- hot ceilings / very hot attic spaces;
- drafts or noticeable air movement;
- high heating or cooling bills without an obvious usage change;
- cold floors over a crawl space;
- dust, smells, or attic/crawl-space air entering occupied rooms;
- HVAC that appears to run excessively while comfort remains poor.

Primary source anchors:

- ENERGY STAR — Identify the Problems You Want to Fix  
  https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/identify-problems-you-want-fix
- ENERGY STAR — Attic Insulation Project  
  https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/attic-insulation-project
- ENERGY STAR — Locating Air Leaks  
  https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/do-it-yourself-guide/locating-air-leaks
- ENERGY STAR — DIY Checks and Inspections  
  https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/identify-problems-you-want-fix/diy-checks-inspections

Editorial note:

These signs justify investigating insulation **and** air leakage. They may also point to duct leakage, HVAC imbalance, windows, or another building-envelope problem. Public content should explicitly say diagnosis comes before adding more insulation.

# 2. Moisture / mold / mildew warning signs

## Homeowner-visible signs

Use these as "investigate now" signals:

- musty or moldy odor;
- visible mold or mildew;
- water stains or wet spots;
- damp or wet insulation;
- condensation on windows, walls, pipes, ducts, roof deck, or foam surfaces;
- peeling / bubbling paint associated with moisture;
- persistently elevated indoor humidity;
- moldy or rotted rafters / floor joists;
- a known roof, flashing, plumbing, drainage, or flooding event;
- crawl-space standing water or persistent dampness.

Primary source anchors:

- EPA — A Brief Guide to Mold, Moisture and Your Home  
  https://www.epa.gov/mold/brief-guide-mold-moisture-and-your-home
- EPA — What Does Mold Smell Like?  
  https://www.epa.gov/mold/what-does-mold-smell
- EPA — Main Ways to Control Moisture  
  https://www.epa.gov/mold/what-are-main-ways-control-moisture-your-home
- EPA — Interactive Mold House Tour  
  https://www.epa.gov/mold/interactive-mold-house-tour
- ENERGY STAR — Getting Started / attic warning conditions  
  https://www.energystar.gov/saveathome/seal_insulate/do-it-yourself-guide/getting-started

Important public wording:

- Mold requires moisture.
- Spray foam is **not** a mold treatment.
- If mold is present, the moisture source has to be identified and corrected.
- Do not imply that visible mold automatically means the insulation caused it.
- Do not imply that simply adding spray foam will cure active roof leaks, plumbing leaks, drainage problems, standing crawl-space water, or HVAC humidity problems.

Humidity guidance:

EPA commonly recommends keeping indoor relative humidity below 60% and ideally around 30–50% when practical.

Use that as general homeowner education, not as a promise that every attic/crawl space must always read inside that range without considering its designed assembly.

# 3. Signs an existing spray-foam installation deserves a closer look

These are especially valuable because many homeowners assume "foam is foam" once it is hidden in the attic/crawl space.

## A. Shrinkage / pull-away / long cracks

What homeowners may see:

- foam visibly pulling away from rafters, studs, sheathing, or framing;
- long cracks at the foam-to-framing interface;
- repeated gaps rather than an isolated small imperfection.

Why it matters:

A cracked or separated air-sealing layer can defeat part of the reason the foam was installed.

Primary source:

- Spray Polyurethane Foam Alliance FAQ — "Can spray foam shrink?"  
  https://www.sprayfoam.org/faqs/

SPFA notes that improperly applied SPF, especially some closed-cell foams, can shrink/pull away; extensive shrinkage should be repaired.

## B. Gaps, voids, missed corners, inconsistent coverage

What homeowners may see:

- visible roof deck / wall substrate where foam should be continuous;
- missed corners / transitions;
- large voids around framing;
- obviously inconsistent foam depth.

Why it matters:

DOE Building America guidance specifically calls for visual inspection to confirm consistent specified depth with no gaps or voids.

Primary source:

- Building America Solution Center — Unvented Conditioned Attic with Spray Foam Below Roof Deck  
  https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/unvented-conditioned-attic-spray-foam-insulation-below-roof-deck

Editorial note:

Do not teach a homeowner to estimate code-compliant R-value from appearance alone. A professional should verify product + installed thickness + assembly requirements.

## C. Poor adhesion / delamination

What homeowners may see:

- foam that appears detached from the substrate;
- areas that lift or separate rather than remain bonded.

Primary source:

- SPFA FAQ — adhesive / delamination issues  
  https://www.sprayfoam.org/faqs/

SPFA says proper substrate preparation matters: surfaces should be clean, dry, and free from oils, dust, dirt, and moisture.

## D. Sticky, friable, discolored foam or persistent unusual odor

What homeowners may notice:

- foam remains sticky;
- foam crumbles / is unusually friable;
- unusual discoloration;
- persistent unusual odor rather than a short-lived post-installation odor.

Primary source:

- SPFA FAQ — odors / improper installation symptoms  
  https://www.sprayfoam.org/faqs/

SPFA specifically identifies persistent unusual odor plus sticky, friable, or discolored foam as reasons to contact the SPF contractor.

Public-content guardrail:

Do not diagnose chemical exposure or health risk from odor alone. If a customer reports health symptoms, direct them to an appropriate medical professional and the contractor/product manufacturer rather than making medical claims.

## E. Foam installed where a moisture problem remains active

What homeowners may see:

- water stains around foam or sheathing;
- known roof leak history;
- wet roof deck / framing;
- soft or rotted wood;
- recurring wet spots;
- crawl-space groundwater / drainage problem that was never solved.

Primary source anchors:

- EPA mold/moisture guidance
- ENERGY STAR attic pre-insulation warning conditions
- Building America spray-foam roof guidance

DOE Building America specifically emphasizes:
- robust rainwater protection;
- dry substrate before foam;
- moisture removal strategy for unvented/conditioned attics;
- clean substrate;
- proper application conditions.

Public rule:

**Fix bulk water first.**

Spray foam should not be presented as a substitute for fixing a roof leak, flashing problem, plumbing leak, bad grading, crawl-space water intrusion, or other bulk-water source.

## F. "Conditioned attic" but the attic stays humid / smells musty / condenses

A sealed attic changes the moisture-control strategy.

Building America guidance says an unvented conditioned attic needs a means of moisture removal, such as controlled ventilation, dehumidification, or appropriate coupling to conditioned house air.

Source:

- Building America — Unvented Conditioned Attic with Spray Foam Below Roof Deck  
  https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/unvented-conditioned-attic-spray-foam-insulation-below-roof-deck
- Building America — Below Deck Spray Foam for Existing Roofs  
  https://basc.pnnl.gov/resource-guides/below-deck-spray-foam-insulation-existing-roofs

Public homeowner message:

> A sealed attic is not automatically a dry attic. The assembly still needs a moisture-control strategy.

Avoid telling every homeowner that they need a dehumidifier. The correct strategy depends on how the attic/HVAC/ventilation were designed.

## G. Missing documentation / fire-protection questions

Homeowner questions worth teaching:

- What exact foam product was installed?
- Was an insulation installation certificate provided?
- What thickness was specified?
- What ignition or thermal barrier is required for this product/application?
- Was the required protective coating/barrier installed?
- Are product data / code-compliance documents available?

Source:

- SPFA homeowner fire-protection / intumescent coating guidance  
  https://www.sprayfoam.org/assets/pdf/SPFA-159%2BIntumescent%2BCoatings%2BMarketing%2BBrochure

Public guardrail:

Do not claim every visible foam application needs the same barrier. Requirements depend on product, location, assembly, and code.

# 4. What the symptom may mean — homeowner triage matrix

| What the homeowner notices | Possible building issue(s) | What ECF should explain / inspect | Do not promise |
|---|---|---|---|
| Upstairs much hotter than downstairs | attic insulation, air leakage, duct loss, HVAC balance | insulation boundary, visible gaps, ducts, attic conditions | that spray foam is automatically the only fix |
| High cooling bills | insulation/air leakage, duct leakage, HVAC, windows, usage | envelope + accessible insulation + duct context | guaranteed savings percentage |
| Musty smell | moisture / mold somewhere in building | source of moisture, crawl/attic conditions, visible growth | that foam kills mold |
| Visible mold / mildew | persistent moisture | leak/humidity source first | that insulation alone is remediation |
| Condensation | high RH and/or cold surface | RH, surface temperature, air leakage, insulation continuity | that more foam alone fixes every condensation issue |
| Crawl-space dampness | ground moisture, drainage, outdoor humidity, plumbing leak | vapor control, standing water, drainage, wood moisture, existing insulation | encapsulation over active water as a cure |
| Water stain under roof | roof/flashing leak or condensation | roofing/water entry before insulation repair | spraying over it |
| Foam pulled from rafters/studs | shrinkage/application defect | extent of separation, air-seal continuity | "normal" if widespread |
| Foam visibly has gaps/voids | missed/incomplete application | continuity + required depth | cosmetic-only dismissal |
| Foam sticky / friable / discolored | possible improper application | isolate/inspect, installer/manufacturer documentation | medical diagnosis |
| Persistent unusual foam odor | possible installation/product issue | installer / product manufacturer involvement | declare toxicity from odor alone |
| Conditioned attic still very humid | moisture-removal / HVAC / air-seal strategy issue | attic conditioning/dehumidification/air pathway | universal dehumidifier prescription |
| Foam looks fine but comfort is poor | problem may be elsewhere | ducts, HVAC, windows, building envelope, thermal imaging/blower-door if available | sell more insulation before diagnosis |

# 5. Proposed public education pages / site modules

These should be reviewed with Casey before public implementation.

## P0-A — "Do I need an insulation / building-envelope inspection?"

Problem-first symptom page.

Sections:

- rooms never feel comfortable;
- high electric bills;
- musty smell;
- visible moisture / condensation;
- crawl-space dampness;
- insulation damaged by water / pests;
- prior spray foam but still uncomfortable;
- visible foam gaps / pull-away;
- "I don't know — I just want a second opinion."

CTA:

**Request an inspection / estimate**

Do not name the solution until after assessment.

## P0-B — "7 signs your existing spray foam should be inspected"

Candidate list:

1. Pull-away / shrinkage
2. Gaps / missed areas
3. Inconsistent coverage
4. Delamination
5. Sticky / friable / discolored foam
6. Persistent unusual odor
7. Moisture / condensation / musty conditions that remain after the attic/crawl space was sealed

Close with:

> These signs do not all mean the foam must be removed. They mean the assembly deserves a closer look.

## P0-C — "Mold, moisture and insulation: what foam can — and cannot — fix"

Essential trust page.

Explain four separate moisture paths:

1. bulk water (roof / plumbing / flood / drainage);
2. ground moisture / crawl-space vapor;
3. humid air + air leakage;
4. condensation at cold surfaces.

Then explain where insulation / air sealing fits into the system.

Use EPA as primary mold/moisture source.

## P0-D — "My attic was spray foamed — why is it still humid?"

Lowcountry-specific educational page.

Explain:
- unvented/conditioned attic basics;
- foam = air/thermal boundary;
- moisture still needs a strategy;
- HVAC / ducts / ventilation / dehumidification can matter;
- roof leaks still matter;
- gaps / voids still matter.

Use Building America as primary source.

## P0-E — "Should I repair, add to, or replace existing spray foam?"

Decision page:

- isolated repair;
- incomplete coverage / top-up;
- defective foam needing contractor/manufacturer review;
- active water issue first;
- no-insulation issue at all — another system may be the real problem.

No removal recommendation without inspection.

## P0-F — "Buying a home that already has spray foam?"

Very useful in the Lowcountry real-estate market.

Homebuyer checklist:
- what product?
- install date?
- contractor?
- documentation/certificate?
- open or closed cell?
- location / thickness?
- ignition/thermal barrier documentation?
- roof leak history?
- attic RH/conditioning strategy?
- visible gaps/pull-away?
- insurance questions?
- pest/termite inspection access?

Do not provide insurance/legal assurances.

# 8. Claims ECF should avoid unless specifically supported

Avoid:

- "Mold means your spray foam failed."
- "Spray foam prevents all mold."
- "Closed-cell foam makes a roof waterproof."
- "Closed-cell spray foam can never be damaged by flooding."
- "Every spray-foamed attic needs a dehumidifier."
- "Every spray-foamed attic should be X degrees."
- "All odors mean toxic foam."
- "A visual inspection alone proves R-value."
- "All visible foam requires an ignition barrier."
- "More insulation always fixes high bills."
- "Spray foam always fixes HVAC imbalance."
- any universal savings percentage;
- any universal re-entry time;
- health diagnosis from mold/odor exposure.

Prefer:

- "may indicate";
- "worth inspecting";
- "one possible cause";
- "the assembly needs to be evaluated as a system";
- "product/manufacturer instructions control";
- "fix active water first";
- "diagnose before choosing the repair."

# 12. Spray Foam Inspection / Second Opinion — candidate service framework

Status: **real capability confirmed; public packaging pending Casey review**

## Why this service matters

Many homeowners already have spray foam and are not shopping for a brand-new installation. Their real question is:

> "Is the foam I already paid for actually doing what it is supposed to do?"

That is a distinct customer problem and should become a first-class service path if Casey approves the public packaging.

## Candidate customer triggers

- buying a home that already has spray foam;
- recent foam installation but comfort/humidity problems remain;
- visible pull-away / shrinkage;
- obvious gaps / voids / missed transitions;
- questionable or inconsistent coverage;
- persistent unusual odor;
- sticky/friable/discolored foam;
- water stains / moisture around a foamed assembly;
- attic still humid after roofline foam;
- crawl space still damp/musty;
- dispute or uncertainty about another contractor's workmanship;
- customer wants an independent practical second opinion before paying for more work.

## Candidate audit outputs — confirm with Casey

Possible customer-facing deliverables to discuss:

- visual inspection of accessible foam;
- photo documentation;
- observed gaps / voids / pull-away / adhesion concerns;
- accessible thickness checks where practical;
- moisture / RH context if ECF actually measures it;
- notes on attic/crawl-space assembly and moisture-control strategy;
- review of product/installer documentation the customer can provide;
- plain-English explanation of likely next steps;
- repair / add-to / remove / monitor / refer recommendation categories;
- written scope for corrective work if ECF is the appropriate contractor.

Do not publish this list as promised deliverables until Casey confirms the actual process.

## Important boundary

The value proposition is:

**independent practical review + clear next-step guidance**

not:

- forensic engineering;
- licensed mold assessment unless separately qualified;
- roofing/plumbing/HVAC diagnosis beyond ECF's actual competence;
- guaranteed code compliance determination;
- legal expert opinion.

## Conversion path

Potential future navigation:

→ Spray Foam Inspection / Second Opinion

Education
→ "7 signs your existing spray foam should be inspected"

Guided Estimate
→ "I already have spray foam and want it checked"

This creates a natural path for homeowners who otherwise would not identify themselves as a "new insulation" lead.
