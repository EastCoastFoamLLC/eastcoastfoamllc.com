# PAGE 3 — 7 signs your existing spray foam should be inspected

## Working title

# 7 signs your existing spray foam deserves a closer look

## Intro

Seeing one of these conditions does not automatically mean the foam must be removed.

It means the installation or surrounding building assembly deserves a closer look.

## 1. Foam pulling away from framing or sheathing

A visible separation between foam and the surface it was applied to can interrupt continuity.

An isolated small imperfection and widespread pull-away are not the same thing. The extent matters.

## 2. Visible gaps, voids, or missed transitions

Look for:
- exposed substrate where coverage should be continuous;
- missed corners;
- incomplete framing transitions;
- obvious holes in the air/thermal layer.

DOE Building America guidance emphasizes consistent application with no gaps or voids.

## 3. Coverage that looks very inconsistent

A homeowner should not try to prove R-value from appearance alone.

But visibly uneven or incomplete coverage can justify checking:
- installed thickness;
- continuity;
- the intended assembly;
- product documentation.

## 4. Foam that is not well bonded

If foam appears detached/delaminated from the substrate, the condition deserves inspection.

Proper substrate preparation, dryness, cleanliness, temperature and application matter.

## 5. Foam that remains sticky, friable/crumbly, or unusually discolored

SPFA identifies sticky, friable, or discolored foam—especially with a persistent unusual odor—as a reason to contact the contractor and investigate.

Do not self-diagnose chemical exposure from appearance alone.

## 6. Persistent unusual odor

A short-lived installation odor and a persistent unusual odor are not the same thing.

A persistent concern should be evaluated by the installer/product manufacturer or another qualified party.

If anyone is experiencing health symptoms, seek appropriate medical advice rather than using a website to diagnose the cause.

## 7. The space is still damp, humid, musty, or condensing

A good-looking foam surface does not prove the whole attic/crawl-space system is working correctly.

Possible contributors include:
- active roof/plumbing water;
- ground moisture;
- humid-air leakage;
- inadequate moisture removal;
- HVAC/duct conditions;
- missing/incomplete air sealing;
- assembly design.

## Closing

**Inspect first. Then decide whether the foam needs repair, more work, no work, or whether another building problem is actually driving the symptom.**

# PAGE 4 — Mold, moisture and insulation

## Working title

# Mold, moisture and insulation: what spray foam can — and cannot — fix

## Intro

If a crawl space or attic smells musty, shows condensation, or has visible mold/mildew, the useful first question is:

**Where is the moisture coming from?**

Insulation and air sealing can be part of the solution, but they are not substitutes for fixing active water.

## Moisture path 1 — Bulk water

Examples:
- roof leak;
- failed flashing;
- plumbing leak;
- flood;
- standing crawl-space water.

Rule:

> **Fix active water first.**

Spraying over a leak does not repair the leak.

## Moisture path 2 — Ground/crawl-space moisture

Moisture can move from soil into the crawl-space environment.

The larger solution may involve:
- drainage;
- standing-water correction;
- ground vapor control;
- air sealing;
- insulation;
- humidity control.

The correct scope depends on the actual crawl-space condition.

## Moisture path 3 — Humid-air leakage

Warm humid outdoor/crawl air can move through gaps and interact with cooler surfaces.

Air sealing can matter as much as insulation.

## Moisture path 4 — Condensation

Condensation occurs when moist air reaches a surface cold enough for water to form.

Examples can include:
- ducts;
- pipes;
- roof/sheathing surfaces;
- other cold materials.

Simply adding more insulation may not resolve the underlying air/humidity condition.

## Mold

EPA's central message is straightforward:

**Mold needs moisture.**

If mold is present, the moisture source has to be addressed.

ECF should not market spray foam as:
- a mold treatment;
- mold remediation;
- a guarantee that mold cannot occur.

## Indoor humidity

EPA commonly recommends indoor RH below 60%, ideally around 30–50% when practical.

That is useful homeowner context, not a universal specification for every attic/crawl-space assembly.

# PAGE 5 — My attic was spray foamed. Why is it still humid?

## Working title

# My attic was spray foamed. Why is it still humid?

## Intro

Spray foam at the roofline can change an attic from a traditionally vented space into an unvented/conditioned or semi-conditioned part of the building envelope.

But:

> **"Foam on the roof deck" and "a well-managed conditioned attic" are not automatically the same thing.**

## A conditioned attic still needs a moisture strategy

DOE Building America guidance says unvented conditioned attics need a means of moisture removal.

Depending on the design, that may involve:
- controlled ventilation;
- conditioning;
- dehumidification;
- appropriate connection to conditioned house air.

There is not one universal solution for every home.

## Other reasons humidity can remain high

### Active water

A roof/flashing leak can still wet the assembly.

### Built-in moisture / wet substrate

Spray foam should be installed on appropriately prepared/dry substrate.

### Air leakage

Uncontrolled air pathways can still move humid air.

### Incomplete coverage

Gaps/voids can affect the intended air barrier.

### HVAC / duct conditions

Duct leakage, equipment operation and room/attic relationships may matter.

### Outdoor / crawl-space moisture path

Humidity may be entering from somewhere the foam does not address.

## What a homeowner can do

If the space:
- smells musty;
- shows condensation;
- repeatedly stays very humid;
- shows water staining;
- or feels different from what the contractor promised,

have the system evaluated rather than assuming the answer is simply "more foam."

# PAGE 6 — Open-cell vs closed-cell spray foam

## Working title

# Open-cell vs closed-cell spray foam: what homeowners actually need to know

## Intro

Open-cell and closed-cell spray foam are both spray polyurethane foam, but they behave differently.

The right choice depends on:
- building assembly;
- climate;
- moisture exposure;
- required R-value;
- available space;
- code/product requirements;
- sound goals;
- project budget.

Neither should be marketed as universally "better."

## Open-cell — plain English

Generally:
- softer / lower-density;
- expands substantially;
- useful for filling irregular cavities;
- more vapor-open;
- often lower R-value per inch than closed-cell;
- may be attractive where sound absorption and cavity filling matter.

## Closed-cell — plain English

Generally:
- denser / harder;
- higher R-value per inch;
- lower vapor permeance;
- can be useful where limited space, moisture exposure, or assembly requirements favor it.

## Why homeowners should care about the assembly

A material that works well in one attic/wall/crawl-space condition may not be the best choice in another.

Questions matter:
- Where is the thermal boundary?
- Where is the air barrier?
- Can the assembly dry?
- What happens if water gets in?
- What code/product requirements apply?
- Is the substrate appropriate?

## ECF approach — draft concept

> **Choose the foam to fit the assembly — not the assembly to fit the foam.**

Casey should approve final wording.

# PAGE 7 — What affects spray-foam cost?

## Working title

# What affects the cost of spray foam insulation?

## Intro

A square-foot price alone rarely tells the whole story.

A real spray-foam scope depends on what is being insulated and what has to happen before, during, and after application.

## Cost factors

### Project area

Attic roofline, crawl space, walls, metal building, commercial application, etc.

### Foam type

Open-cell vs closed-cell can change material requirements and project economics.

### Required thickness / assembly

The project must be designed around actual code/product/assembly requirements.

### Access

Tight crawl spaces, complex roof framing, limited access, and occupied spaces can increase prep/labor.

### Existing insulation removal

Removal may be appropriate when old insulation is:
- wet/damaged;
- contaminated;
- obstructing the intended air-seal strategy;
- otherwise incompatible with the scope.

Do not imply all existing insulation must be removed.

### Prep / protection

Masking, protecting finishes/equipment, controlling overspray, and access prep are part of quality work.

### Substrate condition

Wet/dirty/damaged surfaces can require correction before foam should be applied.

### Coatings / barriers

Some applications/products may require ignition/thermal barriers or protective coatings.

### Job size / logistics

Mobilization matters.

# PAGE 9 — Questions to ask any insulation contractor

## Working title

# 15 questions to ask before hiring a spray-foam contractor

## Intro

A contractor should be able to explain the job in plain English.

Use this checklist whether you call East Coast Foam or someone else.

## Checklist

1. What problem are you trying to solve?
2. Why are you recommending this insulation type?
3. What exact product/manufacturer will be used?
4. Open-cell or closed-cell — and why?
5. Where will the air/thermal boundary be?
6. What installed thickness is specified?
7. Does the assembly need to dry in a particular direction?
8. Are there active roof/plumbing/drainage/moisture issues to fix first?
9. What existing insulation will be removed or left in place — and why?
10. How will the space be protected from overspray/damage?
11. What quality checks will be performed?
12. What thermal/ignition barrier or coating requirements apply?
13. What are the product-specific ventilation/re-entry instructions?
14. What documentation/photos/warranty information will I receive?
15. What is not included in the quote?

## Red-flag behavior

Worth slowing down if a contractor:
- cannot explain why a particular foam/material fits the assembly;
- uses only "best" or "highest R-value" as the reasoning;
- tells you to ignore an active leak/moisture source;
- pressures you to approve a major removal immediately without explaining why;
- will not identify the product or scope;
- gives no written description of what will be done.

## Closing

**A good contractor should make the decision easier to understand, not harder.**

Full removal is one possible outcome, not the automatic first step.

# PAGE 12 — Air sealing vs insulation

## Working title

# Insulation and air sealing are not the same thing

## Draft

Insulation slows heat flow.

Air sealing reduces uncontrolled air movement through gaps, penetrations and joints.

A home can have plenty of insulation and still:
- feel drafty;
- leak conditioned air;
- pull dusty attic/crawl air into rooms;
- have comfort problems.

ENERGY STAR specifically highlights hidden attic/basement air leaks as major contributors to comfort and energy problems.

Spray foam can provide both insulation and air sealing when correctly applied in an appropriate assembly.

But the useful question remains:

**Where is the intended air barrier, and is it continuous?**
